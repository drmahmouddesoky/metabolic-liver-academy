Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to:

  1. Use the five key terms correctly: microbiota, microbiome, probiotic, prebiotic, postbiotic.
  2. Name the main things that shape the gut microbiome.
  3. Describe four jobs the microbiome does in health.
  4. Explain what today's microbiome tests can and cannot tell us.

1Start with a question

A patient hands you a colourful stool microbiome report. It cost a lot of money. It says her "diversity score" is low and lists twelve "bad bacteria". She asks: "What should I eat, and which probiotic should I buy?"

Predict first. Can this report tell you what is wrong with her?

  • A. Yes, it shows the cause of her symptoms.
  • B. Partly. It shows which probiotic she needs.
  • C. Not really. It describes her stool on one day, but it cannot diagnose a disease.

Keep your answer in mind. By the end of this module you will know why.

2The words, and where they come from

  • Microbiota means the living microbes themselves: bacteria, viruses, fungi and others.
  • Microbiome is wider. It means the microbes, their genes and the place they live in. Joshua Lederberg, a Nobel laureate, helped make the word popular in 2001.
  • Probiotic comes from Greek and Latin: "pro" means "for", and "bios" means "life". Today it means live microbes that give a health benefit when taken in adequate amounts (ISAPP 2014 [1]).
  • Prebiotic means a substance that our own microbes use selectively, and that gives a health benefit. Example: some types of fibre (ISAPP 2017 [2]).
  • Synbiotic means a mix of live microbes and a substance they use, with a proven health benefit (ISAPP 2020 [3]).
  • Postbiotic means non-living microbes or their parts that give a health benefit (ISAPP 2021 [4]).

ISAPP is the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics. Its expert panels wrote these definitions.

Key point: "benefit" is part of every definition. A product with live bacteria but no proven benefit is not truly a probiotic.

3How big is it?

  • Most of our gut microbes live in the colon (large bowel).
  • A "reference" adult carries about 38 trillion bacteria. That is roughly the same number as human cells, not ten times more, as old textbooks said (Sender, PLoS Biol 2016 [5]).
  • Together, gut microbes carry millions of genes. That is far more than our own genes (Qin, Nature 2010 [6]).

So the microbiome is less like a passenger and more like an extra organ, with its own chemistry.

4What does it do in health?

Four main jobs:

  • Digests fibre. Microbes ferment fibre we cannot digest. They make short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate. Butyrate is the main fuel for the cells lining the colon.
  • Protects us. A full, healthy community leaves less room and food for harmful germs. This is called "colonisation resistance".
  • Trains the immune system. Early-life microbes help the immune system learn what to attack and what to tolerate.
  • Handles bile acids and makes vitamins. Microbes change bile acids and make some vitamins, such as vitamin K and some B vitamins. The bile-acid job matters for the liver. We return to it in Module 6.

5What shapes it?

  • Early life. Birth by caesarean section or vaginal birth, breast or formula feeding, and early antibiotics.
  • Diet. Fibre feeds microbes. In one large citizen-science study, people who ate more than 30 different plant foods a week had a more diverse gut microbiome than those eating 10 or fewer (McDonald, mSystems 2018 [7]). This is an association, not proof of cause.
  • Medicines. Antibiotics, of course. But also common non-antibiotic drugs. In lab tests, about one in four non-antibiotic drugs slowed the growth of at least one gut bacterium (Maier, Nature 2018 [8]). Proton pump inhibitors (acid tablets) and metformin both change the gut microbiome in people (Imhann, Gut 2016 [9]; Forslund, Nature 2015 [10]).
  • Age and illness. The microbiome changes with age, and with diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease.
Pause and think. If a patient takes acid tablets and metformin, and his microbiome looks "abnormal", what is the first thing to consider? The medicines. We build on this "cause or consequence" idea in Module 2.

6What can we measure, and what can't we?

  • Most tests read bacterial DNA in a stool sample. Some read one marker gene ("16S" sequencing). Others read all the DNA ("shotgun metagenomics").
  • Stool is not the same as the gut wall. Microbes living on the gut lining can differ from those in stool.
  • The microbiome changes from day to day with meals, medicines and illness. One sample is one snapshot.
  • There is no agreed definition of a "normal" or "healthy" microbiome yet.
  • An international expert consensus found not enough evidence to use microbiome testing routinely in clinical practice. It also said test providers should not give treatment advice based on these results (Porcari, Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol 2025 [11]).

Back to the opening question. The answer is C. The report describes her stool on one day. It cannot diagnose her disease, and it cannot pick her probiotic.

7Test yourself

Choose an answer to see the explanation. Nothing is recorded.

1. Which term means the microbes plus their genes and their environment?

Microbiota means the microbes only. Microbiome is the wider term.

2. A product contains live bacteria but has no proven health benefit. Is it a probiotic by the ISAPP definition?

Every ISAPP definition includes a proven benefit.

3. What is the main fuel for the cells lining the colon?

Butyrate is a short-chain fatty acid made when microbes ferment fibre.

4. A patient on metformin and a PPI has an "abnormal" microbiome report. What should you consider first?

Both drugs change the gut microbiome in people.

5. According to the 2025 international consensus, routine microbiome testing in clinical practice is:

The consensus found insufficient evidence and warned against treatment advice from these reports.

8Take-home message

The microbiome is real and important. But we cannot yet read it like a blood test. Diagnose first. Then treat the gut.

9Summary

  • Five words: microbiota, microbiome, probiotic, prebiotic, postbiotic. "Benefit" is part of the definition.
  • About 38 trillion gut bacteria, mostly in the colon. Far more microbial genes than human genes.
  • Four jobs: digest fibre (SCFAs, butyrate), protect against germs, train immunity, handle bile acids and vitamins.
  • Shaped by early life, diet, medicines (including PPIs and metformin), age and illness.
  • Stool tests are a snapshot. They are not yet for routine diagnosis or treatment choice.
