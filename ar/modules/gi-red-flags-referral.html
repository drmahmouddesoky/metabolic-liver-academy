Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to:

  1. List the red flags that need medical assessment before diet changes.
  2. Decide how urgent a referral is: emergency, urgent or routine.
  3. Write a clear referral using SBAR.
  4. Describe how the dietitian, gastroenterologist, pharmacist and nurse share care.
Quick recall. Layan's calprotectin is 420 µg/g. Her CRP is raised and she is anaemic. Does this point to IBS? Answer: No. It points to inflammation. She needs a gastroenterologist.

1The red flags

These findings are not explained by IBS or "dysbiosis". They need medical assessment first (BSG 2021 [1]; NICE NG12 [2]).

  • Blood in the stool, or black stools.
  • Unintended weight loss.
  • Iron deficiency or anaemia without a clear cause.
  • Diarrhoea that wakes the patient at night.
  • Fever, or feeling generally unwell.
  • A new, lasting change in bowel habit in an older adult (many guidelines use age 50 or 60).
  • Family history of bowel cancer, IBD or celiac disease.
  • Persistent vomiting, severe or worsening pain, or a lump in the abdomen.
  • No improvement despite good first-line treatment.

Key point: diet and probiotics must never delay the work-up of a red flag.

2How urgent?

  • Emergency (same day, emergency department): heavy bleeding, fainting, severe pain, signs of bowel blockage (repeated vomiting, swollen belly, no stool or gas), high fever with bloody diarrhoea.
  • Urgent (days to a few weeks): weight loss, anaemia, blood in stool, very high calprotectin, suspected cancer. Follow your local fast-track pathway.
  • Routine: symptoms that do not settle after good first-line care, with no red flags.
Pause and think. Layan has weight loss, anaemia, night-time diarrhoea and calprotectin 420 µg/g. She is stable. Which level? Urgent.

3SBAR – a clear way to refer

SBAR is a short structure for passing on clinical information. Hospitals use it widely to improve safety.

  • S – Situation: what is happening now, in one line.
  • B – Background: key history, medicines, tests.
  • A – Assessment: what you think is going on, and why you are worried.
  • R – Recommendation: what you are asking for, and by when.

Layan's SBAR, written by her dietitian:

  • S: "34-year-old woman referred to me for IBS diet advice. I am concerned this may not be IBS."
  • B: "Eight months of pain and loose stools, some at night. 5 kg weight loss. Smoker. Low ferritin, Hb 10.8 g/dL, CRP raised. tTG-IgA normal with normal total IgA. Calprotectin 420 µg/g."
  • A: "Red flags present. Possible inflammatory bowel disease."
  • R: "Please assess for colonoscopy within your urgent pathway. I will hold any restrictive diet and support her nutrition meanwhile."

4Layan's diagnosis

The gastroenterologist sees Layan within two weeks. Colonoscopy shows ulcers in the last part of the small bowel (terminal ileum). Biopsies confirm Crohn's disease. An MRI of the small bowel shows no narrowing (stricture). Smoking makes Crohn's disease worse [3]. Stopping smoking is one of the most useful steps she can take.

Look back at Module 2. A probiotic and a "gut-healing" diet would not have treated her disease. They would only have delayed it.

5One patient, one team

  • Gastroenterologist: makes the diagnosis, assesses disease activity, starts and adjusts medical treatment, and watches for complications.
  • Dietitian: screens for malnutrition, corrects deficiencies with the team, plans a safe and varied diet, and prevents needless restriction. For most people with IBD, a Mediterranean-style diet is suggested. Texture changes are kept for those with strictures (AGA Clinical Practice Update 2024 [4]). Specialist options such as exclusive enteral nutrition are used in selected patients.
  • Pharmacist: reviews medicines and interactions, checks for drugs that worsen symptoms (for example NSAIDs), and supports adherence.
  • Nurse: educates, follows symptoms, and is often the first to spot a flare.

Shared tools: one referral checklist, SBAR, one joint plan, and agreed points to reassess or escalate.

6Test yourself

Choose an answer to see the explanation. Nothing is recorded.

1. Which finding is a red flag?

Weight loss is not explained by IBS.

2. A patient has repeated vomiting, a swollen belly and no stool or gas for two days. What level of referral?

These are signs of possible bowel obstruction.

3. In SBAR, what goes under "R"?

Recommendation is your clear request.

4. For most adults with IBD, which eating pattern does the AGA 2024 update suggest?

Texture changes are kept for patients with strictures.

5. What did Layan's case teach?

Diagnose first. Then treat the gut.

7Take-home message

Red flags mean stop and refer. Use SBAR to make the referral clear. Then one team builds one plan.

8Summary

  • Red flags: blood, weight loss, anaemia, night-time diarrhoea, fever, new change in older adults, family history, vomiting or severe pain, no response to treatment.
  • Urgency: emergency (same day), urgent (fast-track), routine.
  • SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.
  • Team: gastroenterologist diagnoses and treats; dietitian protects nutrition; pharmacist reviews medicines; nurse educates and follows up.
  • IBD diet: Mediterranean-style for most; texture changes only for strictures.
