The language of lipids: particles, proteins and the master map

What cholesterol and triglyceride are, how lipoproteins carry them, why apoB particles cause atherosclerosis, and how to read a lipid report.

Learning objectives

After this module you should be able to:

  1. Describe the three lipids that matter clinically and what each does in the body.
  2. Name the main lipoprotein classes, where each is made and which apolipoprotein defines it.
  3. Explain why one apoB molecule per particle makes apoB a count of atherogenic particles.
  4. Summarise the genetic, epidemiological and trial evidence that LDL and other apoB particles cause atherosclerosis.
  5. Read a standard lipid report, convert between mg/dL and mmol/L, and know when calculated LDL-C fails.
  6. Use the course's master map to place every lipid-lowering drug on the pathway it targets.

Boxed recommendations are paraphrased from the guideline named on each box, with that guideline's own grades. ESC/EAS and ACC/AHA: Class I = recommended, IIa = should be considered (reasonable), IIb = may be considered, III = not recommended or harmful; Level A = several randomised trials or meta-analyses, B = one trial or large non-randomised studies, C = expert opinion or small studies. Always read the full guideline before applying a recommendation.

1How this course works

This preceptorship follows a molecule of cholesterol from the plate to the artery wall and back. Each module covers one stage of lipid metabolism, shows where medicines act on it, and then turns the biology into decisions at the bedside. The guidance comes from the 2025 ESC/EAS focused update, the 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety guideline and the 2025 Saudi NHC/SHA prevention guideline, set side by side where they differ. [1,2,3]

  • Week 1: the language of lipids (this module) and the exogenous pathway.
  • Week 2: the endogenous pathway and triglyceride-rich lipoproteins; the LDL receptor and PCSK9.
  • Week 3: HDL and reverse cholesterol transport; lipoprotein(a).
  • Week 4: measuring lipids and estimating risk; targets and treatment strategy.
  • Week 5: statins from A to Z; the modern non-statin toolbox and its trials.
  • Week 6: familial hypercholesterolaemia and special populations; lipid care in the Middle East and worldwide, and how to run a lipid clinic.

Look out for four kinds of box. "Where drugs act" ties the biology to a medicine. "For the pharmacist" covers doses, interactions and monitoring. "Advanced" goes deeper, for specialists. "Middle East and North Africa" gives regional data. Guideline recommendations sit in shaded boxes that show the guideline, its class of recommendation and its level of evidence.

2The three lipids that matter

Cholesterol is a rigid four-ring sterol. Every cell uses it to build membranes, and specialised tissues turn it into bile acids (liver), steroid hormones (adrenal glands and gonads) and vitamin D (skin). In plasma about two-thirds of it is esterified to a fatty acid; cholesteryl ester is even less soluble in water and is the form packed into lipoprotein cores. [4]

Triglycerides are three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone. They are the body's energy store and fuel: after a meal they travel from the gut to muscle and fat, and between meals the liver exports them to the same tissues. [4]

Phospholipids have a water-loving head and two fatty-acid tails. With free cholesterol and proteins they form the single-layer surface of every lipoprotein, which lets an oily cargo travel in watery blood. [4]

Clinical pearl

Cholesterol cannot be broken down for energy. The body can only use it, store it as ester, or excrete it, mainly in bile as cholesterol and bile acids. That is why the liver and the gut are the two control points every lipid drug works on.

3The lipoprotein particle

A lipoprotein is a sphere with a core of triglyceride and cholesteryl ester and a surface of phospholipid, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins. Particles are named by density on ultracentrifugation: the more triglyceride, the larger and lighter the particle. [4]

Chylomicrons, VLDL and their remnants are grouped as triglyceride-rich lipoproteins. Particles smaller than about 70 nm (remnants, IDL, LDL and Lp(a)) can cross the endothelium and be trapped in the artery wall. The largest chylomicrons cannot; they cause harm a different way, through pancreatitis when triglycerides are very high. [5]

4Apolipoproteins: the address labels

Apolipoproteins hold the particle together, switch enzymes on and off, and tell receptors where the particle should go. Six matter most in practice. [4,5]

Each VLDL, IDL, LDL and Lp(a) particle carries exactly one apoB-100, and each chylomicron one apoB-48. So the apoB concentration counts the atherogenic particles, however much cholesterol each one carries. [5]

Advanced

Discordance. When triglycerides are high, as in diabetes, obesity or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, LDL particles become smaller and carry less cholesterol each. LDL-C can then look acceptable while the particle count (apoB) is high. This is why guidelines prefer non-HDL-C or apoB in these patients. [2,3]

5Why apoB particles cause atherosclerosis

Three independent lines of evidence agree: genes that raise LDL-C from birth raise coronary risk in proportion; populations with higher LDL-C have more disease; and every drug that lowers LDL-C through the LDL receptor cuts events in proportion to the absolute reduction. The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus calls LDL a cause of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, not a marker of it. [6,7]

The mechanism starts when apoB particles cross the endothelium and stick to proteoglycans in the intima. Retained particles are modified and oxidised, taken up by macrophages that become foam cells, and set off inflammation that builds the plaque. The more particles in the blood and the longer they are there, the more get trapped. [7]

Exposure adds up over a lifetime. One estimate puts the threshold for a first myocardial infarction at about 5,000 mg-years of LDL-C; a person with LDL-C of 125 mg/dL reaches it around age 40. The same 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C is linked to a much larger fall in ischaemic heart disease death when it starts at 40–49 years than at 70–89 years. [8]

Nature ran the trial first. People with loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 have lifelong lower LDL-C and far fewer coronary events, which pointed straight to a drug target. [9] By contrast, genetic variants that raise HDL-C alone do not lower the risk of myocardial infarction, a warning that a lipid number is not always a cause. [10]

Clinical pearl

The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline builds its approach on this idea: risk depends on both the level of apoB-containing lipoproteins and how long a person is exposed to them, so earlier treatment matters. [2,11]

6Reading a lipid report

A standard panel measures total cholesterol, HDL-C and triglycerides. LDL-C is usually calculated, and non-HDL-C is simply total cholesterol minus HDL-C: all the cholesterol in apoB particles. Non-fasting samples are acceptable for most purposes. [8]

Two more tests complete the picture. ApoB is the most direct count of atherogenic particles and is most useful when triglycerides are high or LDL-C is very low on treatment. Lipoprotein(a) is mostly set by genes, so one measurement in adult life is enough for most people; ESC/EAS, ACC/AHA and the Saudi guideline all now advise measuring it once. [2,12,3]

ESC/EAS 2025Class IIaLevel B

Measure lipoprotein(a) at least once in every adult's lifetime to identify people with very high inherited levels. [12]

ACC/AHA 2026Class I

Measure lipoprotein(a) at least once in adulthood; ≥125 nmol/L or ≥50 mg/dL is a risk-enhancing factor (the two units are not interchangeable). [11]

For the pharmacist

Units cause real dosing errors. Before you compare a result with a goal, check whether the report is in mg/dL or mmol/L, and for Lp(a) whether it is in mg/dL (mass) or nmol/L (particle number). There is no exact conversion for Lp(a): about 2 to 2.5 nmol/L per mg/dL is only an approximation. [13]

7The master map

Every later module zooms into one part of this map. Read it from left to right: dietary fat leaves the gut in chylomicrons, lipoprotein lipase strips their triglyceride in the capillaries, remnants return to the liver, and the liver sends out VLDL, which becomes IDL and then LDL. The liver clears LDL through its LDL receptors, and whatever is not cleared can lodge in the artery wall.

Where drugs act

Most LDL-lowering drugs end in the same place: more LDL receptors on the liver. Statins and bempedoic acid cut the liver's own cholesterol synthesis; ezetimibe and resins cut the cholesterol arriving from the gut; PCSK9 drugs stop the receptor being destroyed. Each time the liver senses less cholesterol, it makes more receptors and pulls more LDL out of the blood. Drugs for triglycerides and Lp(a) work elsewhere: on lipoprotein lipase and its brakes, or on the production of apo(a).

8Case: the report that does not add up

A 46-year-old man with a 'normal' LDL-C

Presentation
A 46-year-old man with a BMI of 33 kg/m², type 2 diabetes and MASLD has a fasting panel: total cholesterol 220 mg/dL, HDL-C 32 mg/dL, triglycerides 430 mg/dL. The report gives no LDL-C.
Why no LDL-C?
Triglycerides are above 400 mg/dL, so the Friedewald equation is not valid and the laboratory has rightly withheld it.
What to use instead
Non-HDL-C is 220 − 32 = 188 mg/dL. Measuring apoB would show how many atherogenic particles he carries, which LDL-C would understate here. [8,3]
What comes next
Look for secondary causes (glucose control, alcohol, hypothyroidism, medicines), estimate his risk, and set a non-HDL-C goal. Modules 3, 7 and 8 complete this case.

9Test yourself

Choose an answer to see the explanation. Nothing is recorded.

1. Why does plasma apoB count atherogenic particles?

One apoB per particle means the apoB concentration is a particle count, whatever cholesterol each particle carries.

2. Which particle is least able to enter the artery wall?

Particles larger than about 70 nm cannot cross the endothelium. Very large chylomicrons cause pancreatitis rather than atheroma.

3. A report shows total cholesterol 5.2 mmol/L. What is it in mg/dL?

Multiply cholesterol in mmol/L by 38.67: 5.2 × 38.67 ≈ 201 mg/dL.

4. Genetic variants that raise HDL-C alone…

Mendelian randomisation found no protection from HDL-raising variants alone, one reason HDL-C is not a treatment target.

5. Which statement about lipoprotein(a) testing is correct?

Lp(a) is mostly inherited and stable, so one measurement is enough for most adults. The units cannot be converted exactly.
